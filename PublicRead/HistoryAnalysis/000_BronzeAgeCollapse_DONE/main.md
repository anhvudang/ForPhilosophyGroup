# Bronze Age Collapse

The Bronze Age Collapse (~1200 BC) was not a unique catastrophe but an instance of a universal historical cycle described by Peter Turchin's **Elite Overproduction** theory: too many elites competing for a fixed amount of rent-seeking power destabilizes society until it collapses — through land redistribution (revolution), civil war, or empire-building. All three escape routes create new instability, so collapse is inevitable.

## Bronze made by copper and tin, is the backbone of the economy and the reason/resource for the interconnected world at that point

Bronze was the foundational material of the ancient economy — equivalent to oil today. It is an alloy requiring two ingredients: **copper** and **tin**, which had to be mined in different parts of the world:

- **Tin** was found in Britain, Iberia, Anatolia, and Afghanistan
- **Copper** was mainly found in Cyprus, Crete, and Anatolia

Because no single region had both, producing bronze required global trade spanning from India to Britain. This necessity created the interconnected Bronze Age world — a globalized trading system in which Mycenae, the Hittite Empire, Egypt, Canaan, and Mesopotamia all participated.

Four ways to profit in this world:
1. **Mining** — extracting copper/tin at the source
2. **Manufacturing** — smelting bronze into weapons, tools, and pottery
3. **Trading** — controlling logistics along trade routes
4. **Piracy** — raiding wealthy trading cities

Bronze was the resource that made this world interconnected. When the supply chains and trading relationships collapsed, the entire economic system fell apart simultaneously across multiple civilizations.

## Greek engaged in Piracy (Not rare in history for a country to use piracy as a policy; other examples are British)

Piracy was one of the four primary economic strategies in the Bronze Age world — alongside mining, manufacturing, and trading. For Mycenaean Greeks, piracy was not a fringe criminal activity but a state-level policy for wealth extraction.

Troy, sitting at the center of global trade routes, accumulated enormous wealth from tolls. This made it a perennial target. When the Greeks attacked Troy — as immortalized in Homer's *Iliad* — they were essentially pirates raiding a rich trading hub for its accumulated wealth and resources.

This pattern was not unique to the Greeks. Throughout history, maritime powers have institutionalized piracy as a state instrument — the British use of privateers (licensed pirates) is a later parallel. The Sea Peoples who contributed to the Bronze Age Collapse were themselves a combination of pirates and refugees attacking Egypt out of hunger and opportunity.

Piracy thrived in this world because:
- Trade created concentrated wealth in specific nodes (Troy, Egypt)
- No international enforcement mechanism existed
- Naval capability gave relatively small groups outsized raiding power

## Troy is an extremely important city in the trade network, thus who controls it gains a lot of power

Troy occupied one of the most strategically valuable positions in the Bronze Age world: the gateway between the Aegean Sea and the routes into Anatolia and beyond, sitting at the intersection of land and sea trade connecting Europe, Anatolia, and the Middle East.

To move goods between the Mediterranean world and the interior — Anatolia, Afghanistan, and further east — merchants had to pass through the straits Troy controlled. Troy functioned as the **logistics hub of the ancient world**, collecting tolls from all traffic.

This made Troy extraordinarily wealthy and, therefore, a constant target of warfare and piracy for centuries. Whoever controlled Troy controlled a chokepoint on the most profitable trade routes of the ancient world — making it not just a city but a geopolitical prize.

The Trojan War, far from being merely a story about Helen, reflects this economic reality: Mycenaean Greeks attacked Troy because it represented the greatest concentration of toll-derived wealth in the region.

## The collapse was caused by a "perfect storm" (systems collapse)

The scholarly consensus explanation for the Bronze Age Collapse (~1200 BC) is a **systems collapse** — not one single event but a convergence of multiple destabilizing factors over several decades:

1. **Earthquakes** — Archaeological evidence shows a series of earthquakes across the eastern Mediterranean that physically destroyed infrastructure and destabilized social order.

2. **Climate cooling** — Evidence of a climate shift toward cooler temperatures reduced agricultural yields, making it harder for populations to feed themselves and pay tribute to palace economies.

3. **Internal revolt** — At Mycenae, the palaces were burned down while the surrounding houses remained intact — suggesting destruction came *from within*, not from external conquest. Written palace records confirm the extractive palace economy structure that generated debt and debt-slavery over time.

The "perfect storm" model argues that no single factor would have caused collapse on its own — but together, compounding over decades, they created a cascade that destroyed interconnected civilizations across the eastern Mediterranean simultaneously.

**Caveat:** This theory (scholarly consensus) is presented fairly here, but see the [Elite Overproduction section](#elite-over-production) for the argument that systems collapse is a symptom, not the root cause — and that structural elite overproduction made these societies unable to survive any shock.

## Elite overproduction

Peter Turchin's **Elite Overproduction** theory argues that societies collapse not from the bottom up (popular revolt, as Marx argued) but from the top — through civil war among elites competing for a fixed pool of rent-seeking power.

**How it works:**
- A permanent hereditary elite gives society advantages: organization, wealth, and scale (enabling populations to grow from ~10,000 to ~1,000,000)
- The elite earn through **rent-seeking behavior** — charging others for access to land, trade routes, credentials, or resources they control
- Over time, the elite class grows but rent-seeking opportunities remain fixed → more elites compete for the same power
- Simultaneously, rent-seeking generates **debt → debt-slavery** among the population, creating pressure from below
- The combination makes society structurally fragile — not collapsing on its own, but unable to survive any external shock

**Evidence:**
- Mycenae: palace graves show kings growing wealthier over time; palace records confirm the extractive palace economy
- Maya civilization (~200–1200 AD): same growth-and-collapse arc
- Piketty's *Capital in the 21st Century*: capital grows at 5%/year vs. 2% real economy — the modern mathematical expression of the same dynamic

For the full theory with mechanisms, analogies, and modern parallels, see [Elite Overproduction — Detailed](elite_overproduction_detail.md).

## How to stabilize a system: Land redistribution (revolution), Civil war, and Empire-building

Once elite overproduction destabilizes a society, three historical mechanisms have been used to resolve it — but all three generate new instability:

1. **Land redistribution (revolution)** — A strong leader kills off competing elites, redistributes land, and cancels debts, resetting the system. This is how early kings came to power. But redistribution is itself a revolutionary act that creates new enemies and restarts the cycle.

2. **Civil war** — Elites fight until one faction wins everything. The winner gains control of the rent-seeking apparatus, but the society has been severely weakened and the cycle begins again under the victor.

3. **Empire-building** — Rather than fighting internally, elites agree to expand outward and conquer new territory, creating new resources to distribute. But empire requires constant expansion; when expansion stops, internal competition resumes.

All three are unstable:
- Land redistribution = revolution
- Civil war = self-destruction
- Empire-building = war, and eventual overextension

The conclusion: **no society is permanently stable**. The cycle of elite overproduction, destabilization, and collapse is universal. The question is not *whether* a society will collapse, but *when* — and what comes after.

## The collapse produced progress (Greek Civ)

The Bronze Age Collapse, devastating as it was, produced the conditions that gave rise to Greek civilization — and through it, Western civilization.

**What the collapse created:**
- Mycenae's destruction ended centralized palace economies and left Greece **decentralized, illiterate, and poor** — precisely the conditions that forced innovation
- Loss of the Linear B scribal writing system → adoption of the simpler Phoenician **alphabet** (accessible to everyone, not just palace scribes)
- Emergence of the **polis** (city-state): small, competing, diverse political units that forced constant innovation and democratized political participation
- Conditions for **Homer**: without the collapse and the resulting oral tradition, there would have been no *Iliad* and *Odyssey*

**The parallel in Canaan:**
- Canaan's collapse (part of the same Bronze Age crisis) produced the **Israelites** and ultimately the **Bible**
- The two products of the Bronze Age Collapse — Greek civilization and the Hebrew Bible — became the twin foundations of Western civilization

The analogy: a forest fire burns down old growth but regenerates the ecosystem, producing a stronger, more diverse forest. Preventing collapse prevents the regeneration that makes progress possible. The most creative civilization in history arose specifically *because* the old one was destroyed.
