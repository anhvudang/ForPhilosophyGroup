# Elite Overproduction — Detailed

*Extended notes on Peter Turchin's theory as applied to the Bronze Age Collapse. Summary version: [readme.md](readme.md#elite-overproduction)*

---

## The Theory

Peter Turchin (Russian-American historian) developed **Elite Overproduction** as an alternative to the Marxist model of societal collapse. Marx argued collapse is bottom-up: workers are exploited until they revolt. Turchin argues the historical data shows the opposite: collapse is driven by **infighting among elites** competing for a fixed pool of power.

The core claim: *the problem with society is not too many poor people — it is too many rich people.*

---

## The Permanent Hereditary Elite

Before ~1200 BC, elites existed but were not permanent — status had to be re-earned each generation, and elites could not pass privileges to their children. Around 1200 BC, the **permanent hereditary elite** became the dominant social structure.

**Three advantages it gives society:**

| Advantage    | Mechanism                                                     | Example                                    |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Organization | Elite coordinate large-scale projects                         | Irrigation, pyramids, warfare              |
| Wealth       | Inequality incentivizes labor; coordination generates surplus | Palace economies                           |
| Scale        | Organized wealth enables population growth                    | Societies scale from ~10,000 → ~1,000,000 |

These advantages explain why hereditary elite societies consistently conquered egalitarian ones — not because they were morally better, but because they were more organizationally powerful.

---

## Rent-Seeking Behavior

The mechanism by which elites extract value without producing it. Classic form: a landlord who does no farming but collects a share of crops from farmers who use his land.

**The debt spiral:**
1. Farmer pays rent (portion of crops) to landlord
2. Bad harvest → farmer cannot pay → goes into debt
3. Compound interest → debt grows faster than farmer can repay
4. Outcome: **debt-slavery** — farmer's children owe labor to landlord in perpetuity

Debt-slavery is the **natural endpoint** of rent-seeking behavior. Over time, any society with rent-seeking will accumulate a growing underclass of debt-slaves, creating instability.

**Modern analogs:**
- University degrees as rent: institutions charge access fees to credentials needed for social mobility; learning is secondary to gate-keeping
- Stock market over manufacturing: capital (5%/yr return) consistently outperforms real economy (2%/yr) — Piketty's *Capital in the 21st Century* — so rational actors stop creating wealth and extract it instead

---

## The Overproduction Dynamic

Elite numbers grow over time (hereditary transmission + population growth), but rent-seeking opportunities remain fixed (only so much land; only so many trade chokepoints; only so many palace positions).

**Result:** increasing competition among elites for a fixed pool of power.

This is the "overproduction": the elite class produces more members than the system can absorb. The excess elites have two options — fight each other or extract more aggressively from the population. Usually both.

---

## Why This Explains the Bronze Age Collapse Better Than "Perfect Storm"

The perfect storm model (earthquakes + climate + revolt) describes the *proximate triggers* but not the *structural cause*. Turchin's model explains why:

1. **Earthquakes** happen in all eras — resilient societies survive them; structurally unstable ones do not
2. **Climate cooling** reduces harvests — but who bears that cost? The palace economy ensured the elite kept their cut while the population starved
3. **Internal revolt** — the palace being burned from within (Mycenae evidence) is exactly what elite overproduction predicts

The societies collapsed because their instability made them **non-resilient** — like a tower already on the verge of falling. Any shock, however small, would topple it.

Turchin's model predicts not just *that* they collapsed but *why many collapsed simultaneously*: they all had the same structural disease.

## Cross-Civilizational Evidence

**Maya Civilization (Central America):**
- Growth from ~200 AD → peak ~900 AD
- Collapse ~900–1200 AD
- Same arc: expansion, elite growth, internal competition, collapse
- Not caused by external invasion or single disaster

**Piketty's Modern Data:**
- Capital return: ~5%/year
- Real economy growth: ~2%/year
- Over decades, this mathematically concentrates wealth in fewer hands
- Modern parallel to ancient debt-slavery: financialization, asset inflation, declining labor share

**French Revolution:**
- Not a revolt of the poor — the *petite bourgeoisie* (lower elite) revolted against the upper elite
- Turchin's broader claim: **historical conflict is always between the have-a-lot and the have-somewhat-more**, not between rich and poor
- The poor riot but do not revolt; it is always lower elite who make revolutions

## Why No Society Is Permanently Stable

The three escape valves (land redistribution, civil war, empire-building) all generate new instability:

- Land redistribution cancels current elite power → creates enemies → new cycle begins
- Civil war destroys human and material capital → winning faction restarts elite overproduction
- Empire-building requires constant expansion → when expansion stops, internal competition resumes; empires always eventually overextend

**Therefore:** collapse is not a failure mode — it is the *natural rhythm* of complex societies. The question is not whether to prevent it but what productive conditions the collapse creates.

The forest fire analogy: suppressing fires makes the forest less resilient and guarantees a larger, more destructive fire later. Periodic "burns" (collapses) regenerate the ecosystem and create the conditions for stronger regrowth.

---

## Application to Mycenaean Greece

Direct evidence from the archaeological record:

1. **Grave goods**: Mycenaean royal graves show kings buried with increasingly expensive items over successive generations — direct evidence of growing elite wealth concentration
2. **Palace records**: Linear B tablets document the palace economy's extractive structure — centralized collection and redistribution with the palace taking a cut
3. **Burn pattern**: Palaces burned while surrounding houses survived → internal revolt, not external conquest

The Mycenaean collapse was not a historical accident — it was structurally inevitable given the rent-seeking palace economy and the growing hereditary elite class competing for control of it.
