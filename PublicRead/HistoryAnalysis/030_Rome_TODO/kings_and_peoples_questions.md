# The King's Three Questions (and the People's) — Detailed

*Extended notes on how founders convert conquest into durable rule. Summary version: [main.md](main.md#kings-questions-legitimacy-identity-differentiation). Synthesizes Weber, Anderson, Hobsbawm, and Schmitt with the Roman case.*

---

## The Problem: Violence Seizes Power but Cannot Hold It

Almost every founder and king comes to power by killing — conquest, civil war, the elimination of rivals. Romulus killed Remus; Brutus's Republic was born in revolt; Augustus waded through proscriptions and Actium to reach the throne. But coercion is expensive, brittle, and self-undermining. As the proverb (apocryphal, often attached to Talleyrand) has it: **"You can do anything with bayonets except sit on them."**

The scholarly version is **Max Weber's**: stable rule rests not on force but on *Legitimitätsglaube* — a **belief in legitimacy** among the ruled — which produces order "more stable than that arising from self-interest or habit alone." So the founder's real task, once the killing is done, is to shift from force to *meaning*. He does this by answering three questions — and the genius move is that **answering them for himself also answers them for his people.**

---

## Question 1 — "Why am I king?" = LEGITIMACY

*(Mirrors the people's question: "Where do we come from?")*

**Weber's three types of legitimate authority:**
- **Traditional** — "it has always been so" (hereditary monarchy, custom).
- **Charismatic** — faith in the leader's exceptional personal qualities (the conqueror, the prophet).
- **Legal-rational** — trust in legality and impersonal rules (offices, constitutions).

A conqueror starts with raw charisma (victory) and must **convert it into traditional and legal-rational legitimacy** to last. Other classic legitimacy engines:
- **Divine right** — authority from God, unconditional, hereditary.
- **Mandate of Heaven** (China) — Heaven grants rule to the virtuous, but the mandate is **conditional and revocable**; a successful rebellion *proves* Heaven shifted favor.

**Augustus is the paradigm of manufactured legitimacy.** Having won by force, he staged the *res publica restituta* — "restoration of the Republic" (27 BC) — handing power back to the Senate, which handed it back to him with the honorific **Augustus**. He ruled as *princeps*, "first citizen," never "king," governing through *auctoritas* (prestige) rather than open monarchy. And he claimed **descent from Venus through Aeneas**, with deified Caesar making him *divi filius*, "son of a god." (See [Homer vs. Vergil](homer_vergil_detail.md) for how the *Aeneid* did this work.) William the Conqueror did the same after 1066: a papal banner, a coronation at Westminster, and a claimed promise of the throne turned conquest into lawful succession.

## Question 2 — "Who are we?" = CULTURAL IDENTITY

*(Mirrors the people's question: "What are we?")*

A people must be *imagined* into existence. **Benedict Anderson** (*Imagined Communities*, 1983): a nation is "an imagined political community" — members "will never know most of their fellow-members yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion." This **horizontal comradeship** is what makes millions willing to die for strangers — i.e., it is what enables **collective sacrifice** (Rome refusing to surrender after Cannae).

**Eric Hobsbawm** (*The Invention of Tradition*, 1983): the rituals and symbols that "claim to be old are often quite recent" — invented to foster cohesion, legitimize authority, and solidify shared values. Founders therefore manufacture: founding myths, national epics, shared language, civic religion, and ceremony.

Rome built identity exactly this way — Romulus's **grant of citizenship** turning outsiders into Romans; the founding legends taught as moral catechism; and finally the **Aeneid** as a state-sponsored origin epic. The values *pietas*, *virtus*, and devotion to the *res publica* answered "what are we?": a people who put Rome above self.

## Question 3 — "Who are our enemies?" = CULTURAL DIFFERENTIATION

*(Mirrors the people's question: "Where are we going / what do we strive against?")*

Identity needs an *outside*. **Carl Schmitt** (*The Concept of the Political*, 1932): the defining criterion of the political is the **friend–enemy distinction**. The enemy is the public *hostis* (collective foe), not a private *inimicus*. Crucially, "the existence of a genuine enemy is what gives a political community its boundaries, cohesion, and self-definition. Without an enemy, there is no meaningful 'we.'"

A common enemy consolidates internal solidarity — the in-group is defined against the out-group:
- **Greeks vs. Persians** — the Greco-Persian Wars forged pan-Hellenic identity.
- **Rome vs. Carthage** — "*Carthago delenda est*"; the existential duel that defined Roman virtue (see [the Qin parallel](qin_parallel_detail.md) and [brief history](brief_history_detail.md#iii-conquest-of-italy-and-the-punic-wars-509--146-bc)).
- The "civilized" vs. the **barbarians** everywhere.

## The Synthesis

The founder's three answers map directly onto a people's three existential needs:

| King's question | People's question | Theorist |
|---|---|---|
| Why am I king? (legitimacy) | Where do we come from? (origin) | Weber |
| Who are we? (identity) | What are we? (shared self) | Anderson, Hobsbawm |
| Who are our enemies? (differentiation) | Where are we going / against what? | Schmitt |

The strongest states **align the ruler's legitimacy project with the people's hunger for meaning**. Augustus is the model: a conqueror who fused legitimacy (restored Republic, divine descent), identity (the *Aeneid*, civic religion), and a differentiating mission, converting raw military victory into an order that outlived him by centuries. Weber explains *why* it works (belief, not bayonets, stabilizes rule); Anderson and Hobsbawm explain *how* the "we" is built; Schmitt explains why the "we" needs a "they."

This is also why **Qin China** fell in 15 years while Rome lasted 500 (see [the Qin parallel](qin_parallel_detail.md)): Qin answered the questions for the *ruler* by force, but never made the people *believe* — discipline without legitimacy. Rome made its people believe they were the children of Troy, defined by duty, destined to rule. That belief was the cohesion.
