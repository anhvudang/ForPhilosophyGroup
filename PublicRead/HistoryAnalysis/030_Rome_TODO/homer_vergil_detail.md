# Homer vs. Vergil: The War for the Soul of Rome — Detailed

*Extended notes on how Augustus used the Aeneid to rewrite Rome's soul. Summary version: [main.md](main.md#the-soul-of-rome-homer-vs-vergil).*

---

## Augustus's Three Problems

By 27 BC Octavian had won, but a sword cannot sit on a throne forever (see [the king's questions](kings_and_peoples_questions.md)). He faced three problems, all solvable by one new founding myth:

1. **Legitimacy.** He was a conqueror in a state whose deepest myth — Lucius Brutus overthrowing the last king and founding the Republic — *celebrated killing would-be kings*. That myth had armed his adoptive father's assassins. It had to be displaced.
2. **Identity.** The old Republican values (especially *Libertas*) had curdled into a century of civil war. Rome needed a *new* organizing value.
3. **Greek cultural dominance.** Educated Romans revered Greek culture — Homer, Plato, the philosophers. Rome ruled the Greeks militarily but felt culturally inferior to them.

The solution was **Virgil's *Aeneid*** (written 29–19 BC under Augustus's close supervision) — described in the lecture as "the greatest work of propaganda in human history." It answered all three at once.

## Homer the Educator vs. Virgil the Propagandist

The lecture's central contrast: the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* argue that **love is the basis of civilization**; the *Aeneid* argues that **piety (duty/obedience) is**.

**Homer's gospel of love.**
- In the *Iliad*, Achilles begins as a merciless killing machine. The death of his friend Patroclus drives him to slaughter and mutilate Hector — but the epic's climax is **Priam, Hector's father, kissing the hand of the man who killed his son** to beg for the body. Achilles weeps, sees his own father in Priam, and relents. The warrior becomes capable of *pity* — Homer's image of what makes us human.
- In the *Odyssey*, Odysseus survives by cunning and is ultimately drawn home by **love** — for Penelope, his son, his identity. The brooch and the bow become tokens of recognition and restored selfhood.

**Virgil's gospel of piety.** The *Aeneid* systematically **inverts** these values to serve the Roman ideal of *pietas*:
- Love becomes a **disease** and a trap. Queen **Dido** loves Aeneas; he abandons her to follow his destiny, and she kills herself, cursing his line (the mythic origin of Hannibal and the Punic Wars). Duty *requires* him to cause her death.
- His wife **Creusa** dies during the escape from Troy so nothing distracts him from his mission.
- The famous closing scene: Aeneas kills the **surrendering Turnus** — *without* divine prompting — proving he has fully become the instrument of piety. Mercy yields to duty.
- The **Trojan Horse** is reframed by the lecture as Greek *culture itself* (logic, philosophy, theatre) — the real weapon that destroys from within, which dutiful Rome must resist.

The deeper claim: Western civilization here pivots from the **Greek gospel of love (Homer)** to the **Roman gospel of obedience (Virgil)** — and the lecture argues Christianity will later carry this piety-first program forward.

## How the Aeneid Solved All Three Problems

- **Legitimacy:** The Julii (Caesar's and Augustus's family) are made literal descendants of **Aeneas**, son of the goddess Venus — divine ancestry that trumps any elected office, and a founding story that *predates and outranks* the kill-the-king myth of Brutus.
- **Identity:** *Pietas* — duty to gods, family, and Rome above the self — replaces the dangerous *Libertas* that had justified a century of strongmen. Aeneas, who sacrifices love, comfort, and even mercy for his mission, is the model citizen.
- **Greek culture:** The *Aeneid* was built to **stand beside Homer and replace him in Roman schools** — a Roman national epic that borrowed Greek form to assert Roman superiority of values.

The poem frames **Augustus as the end-point of history**: the **Pax Romana** as a kind of achieved eternity, perfection reached by replacing love with obedience. (Where Rome got the idea of "eternity," the lecture teases, is Egypt — the next subject.)

## Link to the Three Human Questions

This is also Rome answering the people's deepest questions (see [main.md](main.md#people-questions-where-do-we-come-from-what-are-we-where-are-we-going) and [the king's-and-people's questions](kings_and_peoples_questions.md)):
- **Where do we come from?** Troy, through Aeneas, by divine destiny.
- **What are we?** A people defined by *pietas* — duty and devotion to Rome above self.
- **Where are we going?** Toward *Roma Aeterna*, "empire without end" (*imperium sine fine*), the Pax Romana as history's destination.

## Caveats (Contested Readings)

- The *Aeneid* is widely read as **far more ambivalent** about Augustus and empire than "pure propaganda" — the "two voices" tradition hears Virgil *mourning* Dido, Creusa, and Turnus, not endorsing their sacrifice. "Co-authored by Augustus" is not the scholarly view.
- Rome never tried to **destroy** Greek culture; it absorbed and revered it. The "Trojan Horse = Greek culture" allegory is the lecture's invention, not Virgil's text.
- "Pax Romana = the end of history / eternal peace" describes the *ideology*; in fact Rome fought constant frontier wars.
- "The Romans created Christianity to make piety the cornerstone" is a provocative teleology — Christianity arose from Judaism and was persecuted by Rome for centuries before adoption.
