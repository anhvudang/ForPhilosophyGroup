## The Formula

**Power = Mass × Energy × Cohesion**, with the priority order **Cohesion > Energy > Mass**.

- **Mass** = raw resources: population, wealth, territory, technology.
- **Energy** = how hard those resources are mobilized and driven.
- **Cohesion** = how tightly the parts hold together toward one purpose.

The traditional military scorecard counts only Mass (and some Energy). On that scorecard, **Carthage should have beaten Rome**: richer, a superior navy, more trade, the genius of Hannibal. Rome won anyway — because cohesion is the *multiplier*, and a small, perfectly cohered force out-fights a large, fractured one. A huge Mass multiplied by low Cohesion yields little real power.

## Why Rome Had Cohesion: Character, Not Resources

File #14's thesis is that Rome's edge was a **value system** producing devotion, discipline, and refusal to quit:

1. **Limitless manpower through openness.** From Romulus's asylum and the absorption of the Sabines onward, Rome *manufactured* citizens. Allies were bound to send soldiers; in the crisis after Cannae, Rome even freed slaves to fight. Carthage, a merchant oligarchy, hired mercenaries — loyal to pay, not to Carthage.

2. **Refusal to quit.** After **Cannae (216 BC)** wiped out a fifth of Rome's adult men and a third of the Senate, the Senate *refused even to discuss* Hannibal's peace terms. A resource-counting state surrenders here; a cohered one does not.

3. **Identity taught as myth.** *Pietas*, *Libertas*, and *Res Publica* were drilled in through founding stories — Brutus executing his own sons, Horatius at the bridge, Mucius Scaevola burning his hand. To believe these stories *was* to be Roman. Devotion meant sacrificing even your children for Rome.

4. **The enemy's structural blindness.** Carthage, as a commercial culture, could not recognize an existential threat in time. Its Council of Elders undermined Hannibal and denied him reinforcements after Cannae. "Devotion means all or nothing" — and a merchant state hedges.

## The Qin Parallel: Same Lesson, Different Engine

China's **Warring States period** ended the same way the Mediterranean's did: the *less* wealthy, less cultured state won by out-organizing richer rivals.

By the late period (c. 350–221 BC), **Qin** sat on the rough northwestern frontier. Its rivals looked superior on paper: **Qi** (east) was the hub of commerce and learning, home to the Jixia Academy; **Chu** (south) was the largest and among the wealthiest by land and people. Qin's advantage was the **Legalist reforms of Shang Yang** (waves in 356 and 350 BC):

- **Law applied equally**, regardless of rank — punishment for all who disobeyed the ruler's law.
- **Merit-based military ranks** — a ladder of twenty ranks earned by battlefield performance (famously, by enemy heads taken), not birth.
- **Collective responsibility** — households grouped into mutual-surveillance units jointly liable for each other.
- **Abolition of hereditary aristocratic privilege**; the state reorganized around one purpose: **"agriculture and war."**

The result was a disciplined, uniform, totally mobilizable society against decentralized, internally divided rivals. Tellingly, when Shang Yang was executed in 338 BC, **his reforms survived him** — institutional cohesion outliving the man. From 230–221 BC, King Zheng conquered all six rival states in nine years and became **Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor**, then standardized script, currency, weights, and even cart-axle widths, and linked the northern walls into the **Great Wall**.

This is the same headline as Rome: **cohesion and discipline overcome superior mass and wealth.**
