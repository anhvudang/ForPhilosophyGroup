# A Brief History of Rome — Detailed

*Extended narrative from the fall of Troy to the Augustan settlement. Summary version: [readme.md](readme.md#a-brief-history-of-rome). Dates marked [legendary] are traditional; the early stories are oral tradition written down by Livy under the early Empire — "what matters is not whether they are true, but that the Romans believed them."*

---

## I. Legendary Origins (c. 1184 – 753 BC)

Rome tells two founding stories, and both are about exile and destiny.

The first begins with the **fall of Troy** (c. 1184 BC). As the city burns, the Trojan prince **Aeneas** escapes carrying his father on his back and leading his son by the hand — the image of *pietas* (duty to gods, family, and state) that Rome would later make its highest virtue. He wanders to Carthage, where Queen **Dido** loves him; when destiny forces him to leave, she kills herself and curses his descendants forever (the mythic seed of Rome's wars with Carthage). Aeneas reaches Italy, wars with **Turnus**, and founds the line that will become Rome.

The second story is **Romulus and Remus** (753 BC) — twins fathered by Mars, suckled by a she-wolf. Romulus kills his twin in a quarrel over the new city's walls and names Rome after himself. Short of people, he declares Rome an **asylum** welcoming refugees, exiles, and outcasts — and then, needing women, stages the **"Rape of the Sabine Women"**: Roman men abduct the daughters of the neighboring Sabines at a festival. War follows, but the women themselves broker peace, and Romulus **doubles Rome's population by granting citizenship to the Sabines**. This openness — manufacturing a people by absorption rather than blood — becomes Rome's structural superpower.

## II. The Kingdom and the Birth of the Republic (753 – 509 BC)

Rome was ruled by **seven kings** over roughly two centuries, several of them Etruscan. The last, **Tarquinius Superbus ("Tarquin the Proud")**, was a tyrant. The breaking point came when his son raped **Lucretia**, a virtuous noblewoman, who denounced the crime and stabbed herself rather than live dishonored.

**Lucius Junius Brutus** led the revolt that expelled the monarchy and founded the **Roman Republic** (509 BC). His genius was institutional: he took the king's powers and **split them among elected offices** (consuls, the Senate, assemblies) with annual terms and mutual checks — a system designed so no single man could become king again. It lasted ~500 years.

Brutus then proved Roman devotion at terrible cost: when his **own two sons** joined a plot to restore the king, he presided over their **public execution**. Two early heroes seal the founding ethic against the Etruscan king Lars Porsena: **Horatius Cocles** holds the bridge over the Tiber alone against an army, then leaps into the river in his armor; and **Gaius Mucius Scaevola**, captured after a failed assassination, thrusts his right hand into a fire and lets it burn without flinching to show the king that Rome breeds men he can never defeat. Porsena withdraws.

## III. Conquest of Italy and the Punic Wars (509 – 146 BC)

Rome slowly subdued its Italian neighbors. When the Greek king **Pyrrhus of Epirus** (280–275 BC) answered southern Greek cities' call for help, he beat the Romans in battle but at such ruinous cost that he reportedly said another such victory would undo him — the origin of "**Pyrrhic victory**." The Romans simply kept coming, and he withdrew.

Then came the existential duel with **Carthage**, the rich Phoenician merchant empire of the western Mediterranean — the **Punic Wars**.

- **First Punic War (264–241 BC):** Rome, a land power with no navy, *built a fleet from scratch*, lost repeatedly, and still ground Carthage down to become the dominant naval power.
- **Second Punic War (218–201 BC):** **Hannibal Barca** crossed the Alps with elephants and shattered Roman armies, climaxing at **Cannae (216 BC)**, where his double-envelopment annihilated a far larger Roman force — tens of thousands dead in a day, including a third of the Senate. *Rome refused to surrender.* It freed slaves to fill the legions, refused to meet Hannibal in pitched battle, and starved him of support until **Scipio Africanus** carried the war to Africa and won at **Zama (202 BC)**.
- **Third Punic War (149–146 BC):** Decades later, **Cato the Elder** ended every speech with "*Carthage must be destroyed*." Rome besieged the city, razed it, and enslaved its people — total annihilation.

In **146 BC** Rome also sacked Corinth, subduing Greece. Having beaten every rival, the Republic became an **"imperial republic"** — a self-contradiction that would tear it apart.

## IV. The Crisis and Fall of the Republic (146 – 44 BC)

Conquest made a few Romans obscenely rich and many poor: aristocrats seized public land worked by slaves, while citizen-soldiers returned from foreign wars to find their farms gone. The **Gracchi brothers** tried land reform; both were murdered by the senatorial elite (**Tiberius 133 BC, Gaius 121 BC**) — the conventional start of the Republic's fall. The **Social War** (91–88 BC) forced Rome to extend citizenship to its Italian allies. Then **Marius and Sulla** did the unthinkable and marched their personal armies *on Rome itself*; Sulla won, made himself dictator, and invented the **proscriptions** — published kill-lists of enemies (82–81 BC) — before retiring rather than seizing kingship.

Into this came **Julius Caesar** (born 100 BC), nephew of Marius, who survived Sulla's proscription as a teenager. A masterful self-mythologizer, he formed the secret **First Triumvirate** with Pompey and Crassus (60 BC), conquered Gaul (58–51 BC) while sending home dispatches that built his legend, and twice raided Britain. Ordered to disband his army, he instead **crossed the Rubicon (49 BC)** — civil war. He beat Pompey at **Pharsalus (48 BC)**, swept the Mediterranean ("*Veni, vidi, vici*," 47 BC), and won absolute power, offering his enemies an un-Roman **clemency**. As dictator he reformed debt, land, citizenship, and the calendar (the **Julian calendar**, 46 BC) — but his speed and his cult of personality alarmed the old guard. On the **Ides of March, 44 BC**, ~60 senators, led by Cassius and **Marcus Brutus**, stabbed him to death in the Senate.

## V. The Birth of the Empire (44 – 27 BC)

Caesar's will reshaped the world: it gave every citizen money and his gardens as public parks, and named his unknown 18-year-old grand-nephew **Octavian** as adopted heir. The killers had expected the Republic to spring back; instead, the people's love and guilt transferred to the heir. Octavian, **Mark Antony**, and Lepidus formed the **Second Triumvirate (43 BC)**, proscribed their enemies (murdering the orator **Cicero**), and crushed Brutus and Cassius at **Philippi (42 BC)**.

The victors then split the world and turned on each other. Antony took the East, allied with **Cleopatra** of Egypt, and squandered his standing; Octavian used Antony's will as a pretext for war and, with his great admiral **Agrippa**, destroyed Antony and Cleopatra's fleet at **Actium (31 BC)**. The lovers killed themselves (30 BC). Octavian stood alone.

## VI. The Augustan Settlement and Aftermath (27 BC – AD 37)

In **27 BC** Octavian theatrically "restored the Republic" to the Senate, which handed it all back and named him **Augustus** ("the revered one"). He ruled as *princeps* — "first citizen," never "king" — disguising one-man rule as restoration. He made **Egypt his private estate** to fund a professional standing army loyal to him, created the **Praetorian Guard**, and began the **Pax Romana**. He also commissioned **Virgil's *Aeneid*** as a new founding myth (see [Homer vs. Vergil](homer_vergil_detail.md)).

His one failure was succession. His adoptive-heir scheme unraveled: he settled on the dour **Tiberius**, under whom the popular **Germanicus** died (AD 19), and the system gave way to the disastrous **Caligula** (AD 37). The lecturer's provocative claim: in spirit the Empire "died" here, then coasted ~300 more years on sheer size and inertia.
