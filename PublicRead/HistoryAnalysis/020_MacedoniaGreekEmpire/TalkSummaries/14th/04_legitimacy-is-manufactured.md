# 4. Legitimacy is manufactured, not discovered

← [Back to Talk 14 README](README.md)

> Quotations are translated from the Vietnamese transcript. Original wording: [04_tinh-chinh-thong-duoc-che-tao_vi.md](04_tinh-chinh-thong-duoc-che-tao_vi.md)

---

## The idea

Every government's legitimacy is extremely shaky if you look into it. But that does not matter — because legitimacy is not a fact requiring proof. It is a **belief requiring production**.

Vũ's closing line:

> *"Even today, the legitimacy of every government is extremely shaky. But that doesn't matter — all that is needed is for everyone to believe it has legitimacy... All that is needed is for everyone to believe those events are real, and then they are real."*

---

## Three concrete operations

The talk offered three examples, and what makes them good is that they sit at three different levels of sophistication.

### 1. Stealing Alexander's corpse (crude, physical)

After Alexander died, the empire split three ways. The general in **Egypt** was the richest but had the worst legitimacy problem — *"why am I king?"* He launched an expedition into the neighbouring territory **not to take land but to take Alexander's body**. And he succeeded.

The logic, reconstructed in the talk:

> *"Everyone already believes Alexander had legitimacy. Something must have helped me to obtain this body, or I wouldn't have obtained it — therefore I am the one helped by God, therefore I have legitimacy."*

Vũ commented on how persuasive this is: *"to us today it makes no sense, like — who could believe this? But back then this did make sense."*

### 2. The temple at Siwa (intermediate, symbolic)

When Alexander came down into Egypt — which surrendered more or less unconditionally — he made a side trip to the **Siwa** oasis to meet the oracle of Zeus-Amon. And the oracle declared: **Alexander was not Philip's son but the son of Zeus.**

Vũ did not pretend to know what actually happened: *"we don't know whether it really went that way or whether Alexander said it did — that we can never know."* Which is the right way to present it.

A secondary detail worth keeping: Egypt did not worship Zeus, but they had the god **Amon**, and they treated Amon and Zeus as one. Vũ took this as evidence that Greek influence on Egypt was already fairly strong — *"roughly shared, some overlap."*

**And this precedent gets reused.** Vũ noted: *"this trip to Egypt — later, everyone who wanted to rule Europe did it. One is Napoleon and the second is Hitler. Both of them had a period of activity in Egypt, and it's simply 'I am Alexander reincarnate.'"*

### 3. The Library of Alexandria (sophisticated, infrastructural)

This is the cleverest operation, because it does not look like propaganda.

The objective: *"it takes all the texts written by Aristotle from Athens and stores them here. They always wanted to create the oldest archival copies and the most canonical copies, store them here, and then spread it out."*

How they got them — the detail that got a laugh from the room:

> *"They went to Athens because they had too much money and said: hey, lend us this pile of texts, we'll go transcribe them, I'll write them out and then return them to you. And to guarantee I'll return them, here's a pile of gold, this will be like a deposit. Then they take them home and... well, bye bye, you keep the gold."*

Why this is legitimacy and not just collecting: if the **oldest** and **most canonical** copies of the whole of Greek knowledge sit in Alexandria, then Alexandria is the centre of the Greek world. Nobody has to declare it.

And it composes with the mechanism in [file 01](01_systematization-as-engine.md): the Library was both a legitimacy machine and the second round of systematization.

**What about the Library burning?** The question came up in the talk. Vũ's answer: it doesn't matter — *"it only needed enough time to spread the idea out."* Infrastructure that burns after replication is complete is a loss of originals, not a loss of influence.

---

## The general mechanism: "hire a poet"

Vũ described the full procedure in its baldest form:

> *"So that's done, he has the body, he'll hire some poet to write a really good piece, and then propagate it to everyone, and then everyone believes it — and he will have legitimacy."*

Three steps: **an interpretable event** → **a good storyteller** → **repetition**. No step requires the event to be true.

And precisely because truth is not required, Vũ extends it to literature and history:

- **Homer:** *"Achilles and Odysseus are fictional characters, but that doesn't matter — all that's needed is for everyone to believe in it."*
- **Rome:** *"Rome talks about battles where it lost up to 80% of its soldiers, say, and then Rome still came back and dominated everything. Whether that's true nobody knows, but people believe it is true, so it is true — and from that it produces the legitimacy of the state."*

---

## And us

The least comfortable part of this idea is that Vũ did not stop at history:

> *"These actions — we'll see them soon enough. Trump will also do many actions that are absurd to an ordinary person, but he leans on those and he can produce legitimacy. Generally speaking, today we do the same thing, it's just shaped to fit our way of thinking better. But in essence we are no different from them at all."*

This is the sharp point: we have not escaped the mechanism, we have only swapped out the set of *things that sound reasonable*. Zeus no longer does the job, but statistics, credentials, awards and "science has proven" do. Same machine, different fuel.

### Nhật's objection — and where it is right

Nhật did not dispute the mechanism; she disputed the **scope of application**:

> *"I also think that in that era, an individual would not think on the basis of an individual's free reason, but would generally rely on the thinking of the whole village, the whole commune, the whole family, and it relates heavily to religion as well as to their historical characteristics... so the limit of Greek culture is that it will run into walls like that — walls of language, walls of religion, walls of ancient culture."*

And she identified the methodological flaw in the question: *"that's where the question is a bit hard — it's placing an adult of the internet generation into it, rather than a person of that era."*

Vũ conceded, and conceded correctly: *"okay, then it isn't [the whole village] choosing to believe, it's only some people inside it who choose to believe — so the cultural wall is still there."*

The sensible conclusion after this exchange: **propaganda does not convert a population, it converts an elite** — which is precisely [file 07](07_ruling-through-the-elite.md).

### Thái's question, unanswered

Thái posed a tightly sealed problem: three kings, three propaganda messages that are **identical**, differing only in name. Who do you believe?

Vũ tried two routes:
1. *Whichever system explains best* — but Thái had pre-closed that; they are identical.
2. *The consistency of the information cluster, and the order of arrival* — *"if you were brainwashed this way first, you'll hold these beliefs first, and other information that contradicts them will have a very high rejection rate."*

The most plausible answer did in fact get said, but as an aside: *"most likely it'll be the nearest one... the one they know most about, and the one who can protect them, who doesn't behead them."*

That is: when content is indistinguishable, people choose by **power and proximity**, not by content. That is a complete answer and it fits everything else in the talk — but it was never raised to the status of a conclusion.

---

## Why this idea is worth keeping

Because it converts a hard question into a workable one. Instead of asking "is this government legitimate?" (hard, prone to endless argument), ask:

**By what mechanism is this legitimacy being produced, and who is paying the poet?**

And more importantly: this idea does not let you exempt yourself. If *"in essence we are no different from them at all,"* then your own set of "sounds reasonable" is also something to inspect.

## Verification notes

- **Stealing Alexander's corpse:** Ptolemy I really did seize Alexander's body and bring it to Egypt — a historical event, not speculation. Keep.
- **Siwa:** the trip to the oracle at Siwa is attested; the *content* of the prophecy survives only in secondary sources. Vũ presented the uncertainty at the right level.
- **The Library "borrowing" without returning:** there is an ancient anecdote about Ptolemy III keeping the originals of the Athenian plays, returning copies, and forfeiting the deposit. Vũ tells the anecdote faithfully, but remember it is an anecdote — rather a neat specimen of the very phenomenon this file is about.
- **"Everyone who wanted to rule Europe went to Egypt to claim to be Alexander reincarnate":** Napoleon had an Egyptian campaign (1798) and did compare himself to Alexander — that part has a basis. The Hitler part is **weak**: the North African campaign was Rommel's and the motives were military and oil, with no evidence of an "Alexander reincarnate" motif. Treat "everyone who wanted to rule Europe did this" as a suggestive pattern, not a law.
- **Rome losing 80% and coming back:** Vũ used this as illustration without naming a battle. Cannae (216 BC) is the nearest case in spirit.
