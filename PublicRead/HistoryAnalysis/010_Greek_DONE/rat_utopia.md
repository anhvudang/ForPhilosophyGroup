# Rat Utopia — Detailed Notes

*Supporting detail for [Greek Civ — Rat Utopia and the Peloponnesian War](readme.md)*

## The Experiment

In the 1960s–70s, American researcher **James B. Calhoun** ran a series of experiments on rats to answer the question: what happens to a society in a world of abundance?

He built enclosed environments with unlimited food, unlimited water, and no predators. Rats could do whatever they wanted. The only intervention was the experimenters feeding them every day.

**Expected outcome**: thriving, happy rat society.
**Actual outcome**: total social collapse and eventual extinction.

### The sequence of breakdown

Rat social behavior is normally highly ritualized. A typical mating sequence:
1. Female rat comes into heat, moves through the colony
2. Male spots her, climbs a mound where she can see him, and begins to dance
3. Female approaches, they chase each other playfully
4. She retreats to her burrow; he waits outside
5. They repeat the chase multiple times until she allows him to catch her
6. They mate and form a family unit

In rat utopia, these rituals collapsed:
- Males stopped dancing; they simply chased and raped females on sight
- Gang rape became common (multiple males, one female)
- Males attempted to break into family burrows to attack the females inside
- Fathers initially defended their families, then abandoned them under the pressure
- Mothers, left unprotected, became traumatized and started attacking their own offspring
- Infant mortality rose sharply; mothers kicked young out of nests prematurely
- Eventually all social cohesion collapsed; the colony died

The experiment was repeated many times. Each time, the same sequence played out.


## One of the Explanation: Status-Locking

The real cause is **status**. In a world of abundance, old animals and people don't die. They live much longer. This blocks the ascent of younger animals/people into positions of power and status.

Imagine a line of people waiting to climb a mountain. Normally, people at the top eventually move off (die, retire), and the line keeps moving. Now imagine the line freezes — the people at the top won't leave. Those waiting have endless energy and nowhere to direct it. They become agitated, then violent. They start attacking the people next to them — not for resources, just from the frustration of trapped potential.

This is what happens in abundance:
- The elite live longer and hold their positions
- Younger elites have nowhere to go
- Their energy turns into irrational, self-destructive violence

## Application to the Peloponnesian War

Athens in the 5th century BC was the wealthiest Greek city. The Delian League tribute gave it enormous revenue. Pericles used this wealth to fund public works, pay citizens for jury duty, and build the Parthenon.

But this wealth froze the social hierarchy. The upper nobility of Athens held their positions indefinitely. The lower nobility — men with ambition, talent, and military service — could not rise.

**The war became an outlet.**

Two figures illustrate the mechanism perfectly:

**Cleon** (Athens) — a lower-noble demagogue who proposed the Mytilenian Decree (execute all adult males of a rebellious ally) and a strategy for winning the war that could actually have worked. He died "very conveniently" in battle at Amphipolis (422 BC) — just as his strategy was on the verge of changing the social order.

**Brasidas** (Sparta) — a Spartan general of non-top-nobility who proved to be the most effective commander of the war. He was popular with the lower classes and threatening to the Spartan establishment. He also died at Amphipolis, the same battle, the same day as Cleon.

Both men had viable winning strategies. Both died the moment their success would have disrupted the status quo. The establishment on both sides benefited from their deaths.

### The War's End Proves the Point

After 27 years of devastating warfare, Athens surrendered in 404 BC. Sparta had won.

What happened to Athens? **Nothing.** 

The Greeks and the Persians all expected Sparta to do what conquerors do — kill the men, enslave the women. Sparta refused. Athens was left intact.

One explanation: balance of power (destroy Athens, Persia grows too strong). Another: the upper nobility of Sparta and the upper nobility of Athens were actually friends — they intermarried, socialized, and respected each other. They had fought this war because the lower nobility demanded it, not because they wanted to destroy each other.

The world in 404 BC was politically identical to 431 BC. Only the young people were dead.

## Broader Historical Pattern

This pattern — conflict between the have-a-lot and the have-somewhat-more — is the engine of most historical upheaval:

- **French Revolution**: driven not by the destitute poor but by the petite bourgeoisie — educated, ambitious people locked out of the nobility
- **Modern revolutions**: typically driven by the middle class, not the working poor
- **Athens' democracy**: was a contest between upper nobility (Pericles' faction) and lower nobility, using the masses as a voting bloc

The poor riot; they don't revolt. The people who actually overturn power structures are those close enough to the top to see what they're missing — and blocked from getting there.
