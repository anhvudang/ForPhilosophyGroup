## III. Second Punic War

Focus on

**Central thesis:** Rome became the great Mediterranean power not because of manpower, technology,
or wealth (the "traditional" military criteria — which would have favoured Carthage), but because
of its **character / value system**, which produced superior *cohesion, discipline, and devotion*.

**Main points and their evidentiary events:**

1. **Rome's openness to citizenship gave it limitless manpower.**
   *Evidence:* Romulus welcoming immigrants and doubling Rome's population after the Sabine episode (c. 750 BC); allies obligated to send soldiers; freeing slaves to fight Hannibal (216–202 BC).

2. **Rome wins by refusing to quit and absorbing horrific losses.**
   *Evidence:* Pyrrhus's "Pyrrhic victories" (280–275 BC); the Senate rejecting Hannibal's peace offer after **Cannae** (216 BC) despite losing ~20% of adult males and a third of the Senate.

3. **The three pillars of Roman identity — *piety, Liberty, Republica*.** These are taught through
   *history-as-mythology*, so that "believing this history is what makes you a Roman."
   *Evidence:* Founding legends (Romulus/Remus 753 BC; the Sabine women); **Lucius Brutus** founding the Republic and executing his own sons (509 BC); **Horatius Cocles** at the bridge and **Mucius Scaevola** burning his hand (c. 508 BC). Devotion = sacrificing even your children for Rome.

4. **The Republic separated the king's five powers into elected institutions** — a stable system lasting ~500 years (and copied from the Etruscans).
   *Evidence:* Brutus's institutional reform after overthrowing Tarquin (509 BC).

5. **Carthage lost because, as a merchant culture, it could never recognize an existential threat.**
   *Evidence:* The Council of Elders undermining Hannibal; Hannibal's lack of logistical support after Cannae; Cato's visit and the destruction of Carthage (149–146 BC) — "devotion means all or nothing."

6. **Methodological caveat:** the stories are mostly oral tradition written down by **Livy** under the early Empire; "we don't believe these stories to be that true… what matters is what they believed."

## IV. The Crisis & Fall of the Republic

Focus on

1. **The Republic became a self-contradicting "imperial republic" after 146 BC**, and its three values now generated corruption, inequality, and "elite overproduction" (Turchin) — optimates vs. populares.
   *Evidence:* Post-Punic land seizures by the rich; the poor losing land while drafted to Spain/Syria; the **Gracchi** assassinations (133 & 121 BC); the **Social War** (91–88 BC); **Marius vs. Sulla** civil war and Sulla's **proscriptions** (82–81 BC).

2. **Caesar cultivated his public image from youth — facts mattered less than appeal.**
   *Evidence:* The embellished **pirate kidnapping** (75 BC); dispatches from Gaul read aloud in the Forum; "*Veni, vidi, vici*" (47 BC).

3. **He was a mythmaker because he was simultaneously general + politician + administrator** — a combination enabled by imagination, making him unpredictable and able to read opponents.
   *Evidence:* Giving up a Triumph for the consulship (59 BC); the secret **First Triumvirate** (60 BC); the **Gallic Wars** (58–51 BC) and **Britain** invasions (55 & 54 BC); the **Julian calendar** (46 BC).

4. **His "clemency" was both strategy and a break with Roman norms** (Romans did not value mercy).
   *Evidence:* Crossing the **Rubicon** (49 BC); clemency in Spain (49 BC); **Pharsalus** (48 BC); the reforms of 44 BC.

5. **He did not actually want to be king; he wanted to "make Rome great again" like a more merciful Sulla** — but his speed of reform disrupted Roman identity.
   *Evidence:* The 44 BC reforms (land, debt, calendar, citizenship) and cult of personality, set against the model of Sulla retiring rather than seizing kingship.

6. **He was killed by friends, not enemies** — because reform creates cognitive dissonance.
   *Evidence:* The conspirators Decimus Brutus, Cassius (a pardoned Pompeian), and Marcus Brutus (whom Caesar treated as a son); the **assassination** (15 March 44 BC).

## V. The Birth of the Empire

Focus on 

**Central thesis:** **The death of Caesar is what made Octavian emperor.** Killing Caesar converted
Caesar's *myth* into *reality* (proving he never wanted to be king), and the people's love and guilt
were transferred onto his heir.

**Main points and their evidentiary events:**

1. **Caesar's will reshaped the board.** Money to every citizen + parks; an unknown 18-year-old (Octavian) named heir; a conspirator (Decimus Brutus) named secondary heir.
   *Evidence:* Antony's reading of the will (44 BC); Octavian borrowing to honour it.

2. **Octavian was not lucky, brilliant, uniquely ruthless, or a great general — yet he won.** Five common explanations are each shown insufficient; the real engine is the converted myth.
   *Evidence:* The "Game of Thrones" of 44 BC; Octavian losing battles but relying on **Marcus Agrippa** (e.g., **Actium**, 31 BC). Modern analogies offered: Justin Trudeau, George W. Bush (inherited political "love").

3. **Caesar's assassination was psychologically explicable through Roman taboos** — the *pomerium* (no weapons/violence in sacred Rome), the sanctity of the Senate and of Caesar's body, and personal obligation (he appointed most senators).
   *Evidence:* Of ~60 conspirators only ~5 could bring themselves to stab him; the first blow was a trembling "pin prick" (origin of "backstabbing"). This same taboo proves *Caesar himself didn't want kingship* — the ultimate taboo.

4. **The conspirators and Antony then self-destructed.** Marcus Brutus could not act (acting would make *him* the would-be king); Antony self-destructed trying to "out-Caesar" Caesar.
   *Evidence:* Brutus refusing to aid Decimus Brutus; Antony's failed **Parthian** campaign, **Cleopatra**, and his will (34–32 BC) used as Octavian's pretext; the **Second Triumvirate** and proscription of **Cicero** (43 BC); **Philippi** (42 BC); **Actium** (31 BC); the suicides (30 BC).

5. **Augustus built durable one-man rule disguised as restoration**, then failed at succession.
   *Evidence:* Egypt as private estate, professional army, Praetorian Guard; declared **Augustus** (27 BC); 40-year reign; the adoptive-succession scheme wrecked by **Tiberius**, leading to **Caligula** (AD 37) — "the death of the Roman Empire" in spirit.

2. **Augustus faced three problems — legitimacy, identity, and Greek cultural dominance** — all solvable by a new founding myth.
   *Evidence:* The Julii's claimed descent from **Aeneas** of Troy (planted by Caesar); the need to suppress the Lucius-Brutus "kill all kings" myth that motivated the assassins; Greek prestige (Homer, Plato, etc.).

4. **The *Aeneid* inverts every Homeric value** — love becomes a disease, imagination becomes destructive, and duty/obedience triumph.
   *Evidence:* The Trojan Horse reframed as "Greek culture" (logic, philosophy, theatre) as the real weapon; Neoptolemus killing Priam; Aeneas's wife and **Dido** dying so he can fulfil destiny (Dido's curse → Hannibal); Aeneas killing the surrendering **Turnus** *without* divine prompting — proof he has become the embodiment of piety.

5. **The Aeneid frames Augustus as the end point of history** — the **Pax Romana** as "eternity" and perfection, achieved by replacing love with piety/obedience.
   *Evidence:* The end of the civil wars under Augustus; the closing claim that this same piety-first program will later be carried by **Christianity**, and that Rome borrowed "eternity" from **Egypt** (the next lecture's subject).
