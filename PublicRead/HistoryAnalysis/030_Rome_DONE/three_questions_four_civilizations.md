# Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going? — Four Answers

*Extended notes answering Gauguin's three questions for four worldviews. Summary version: [readme.md](readme.md#people-questions-where-do-we-come-from-what-are-we-where-are-we-going).*

---

## The Frame: Gauguin's Three Questions

In 1897–98, broke, sick, and grieving his dead daughter in Tahiti, Paul Gauguin painted a mural-scale canvas (139 × 375 cm, now in Boston) and titled it with three questions from his boyhood catechism: ***Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?*** He intended it as the summation of his life's thought, then attempted suicide. The painting is read **right to left** — a sleeping infant (*birth/origin*), figures in the prime of life plucking fruit (*the present, what we are*), and an old woman beside a blue idol of "the Beyond" (*death/destiny*).

The three questions endure as a framework because **every worldview must implicitly answer all three**: an origin (cosmology), a nature/value (what makes life meaningful), and a telos (where it all ends). Below, four civilizations answer the same three questions — and the deepest fault line between them turns out to be **cyclical vs. linear time**.

---

## 1. Classical Greek (Homeric + Athenian)

**Where do we come from?**
From the cosmos itself. Hesiod's *Theogony*: first **Chaos** (the primordial gap), then **Gaia** (Earth), then generations of gods — Ouranos → Kronos and the Titans → **Zeus and the Olympians**. There is *no single creator*; the gods emerge *from* nature rather than standing above it. Athenians added **autochthony** — the claim to be "sprung from the soil" of Attica, grounding citizenship in the land. Humanity descends through declining **ages** (Golden → Silver → Bronze → Heroic → Iron), implying a greater past.

**What are we?**
**Mortals** (*thnetoi*) — defined precisely by our contrast with the deathless gods. The good life is the pursuit of **aretē** (excellence — being the best at what you are) toward **eudaimonia** (flourishing). The Homeric hero craves **kleos** (undying glory) and **timē** (honor); the Athenian adds **reason** — Protagoras's "**man is the measure of all things.**"

**Where are we going?**
To a bleak **Hades** — a realm of bloodless **shades** stripped of strength and mind. Achilles' ghost says he'd rather be a living servant than king of the dead. Because the afterlife is pallid, **real immortality is earthly**: through *kleos* (being sung by poets), through children, through being remembered. Time is broadly **cyclical** — seasons, generations, recurrence — not aimed at a goal.

## 2. Roman

**Where do we come from?**
From **Troy**, through **Aeneas** (Virgil's *Aeneid*), and from **Romulus**, by **divine destiny**. In *Aeneid* Book 1, Jupiter promises Rome *imperium sine fine* — "empire without end." (How Augustus engineered this myth: [Homer vs. Vergil](homer_vergil_detail.md).)

**What are we?**
A people defined by **pietas** (duty to gods, family, state), **virtus** (courage/excellence), and **gravitas** — and oriented to the **collective**: the individual exists for the **res publica**. The highest good is self-sacrifice for Rome (*dulce et decorum est pro patria mori*). Religion is *orthopraxic* — right ritual to keep the *pax deorum* — not creedal.

**Where are we going?**
To **Roma Aeterna** — "Eternal Rome," the empire as the permanent, near-sacred end-point of history; the **Pax Romana** presented as universal order achieved. Personal afterlife is vaguer than the Greek, with weight falling on **ancestor veneration** (the *Manes*, the continuity of the *gens*) and the possible **apotheosis** of great men. Rome's distinctive move: it locates "eternity" not in an afterlife but in **the state itself**.

## 3. Early Christian (1st–4th c. AD)

**Where do we come from?**
From **one transcendent God** who created all things, with humans made **in His image** (*imago Dei*, Genesis). And from **the Fall** — sin enters through Adam, disordering the human condition (later: Augustine's original sin).

**What are we?**
**Fallen sinners** possessing an **immortal soul**, *redeemable through Christ*. The supreme values are **agapē** (self-giving love) and **faith**. Christianity **inherits and transforms** its predecessors: the **Platonic immortal soul**; the **Logos** (John 1, a Greek philosophical term made flesh); and Roman **universalism and piety** — *pietas* reframed as obedience to God, Rome's institutional reach reborn as the church's catholicity. *(How much is Greek "influence" vs. independent development is itself debated.)*

**Where are we going?**
Along a **linear, eschatological** arc: Creation → Fall → Incarnation → **resurrection of the dead** → **Last Judgment** → **eternal life in Heaven** or **damnation in Hell** → the **Kingdom of God**. Salvation is the *goal of history* — sharply **anti-cyclical**.

## 4. Persian (Zoroastrian / Achaemenid)

**Where do we come from?**
From **Ahura Mazda** ("Lord of Wisdom"), the uncreated, wholly good creator, working through the **Amesha Spentas**. The creation is **fundamentally good** — but assaulted by an independent destructive principle.

**What are we?**
**Free moral agents** in a cosmic war — the first major worldview built on **ethical dualism plus free will**. The battle is **Asha** (truth, order) vs. **Druj** (the Lie, chaos); **Spenta Mainyu** vs. **Angra Mainyu / Ahriman**. The whole ethic compresses to three words: **good thoughts, good words, good deeds** (*Humata, Hukhta, Huvarshta*). Each person's choices materially help good win.

**Where are we going?**
Along a **linear eschatology** that strikingly prefigures the Christian one. At death, individual judgment at the **Chinvat Bridge** (the righteous cross, the wicked fall; an intermediate state for the balanced — and hell is **reformative, not eternal**). History climaxes in **Frashokereti**, "the making wonderful": a **Saoshyant** (savior) raises the dead in **bodily resurrection**, all pass through an ordeal of molten metal, **evil is annihilated**, and the world is **restored to perfect, immortal goodness**.

**Contested scholarship — flag clearly.** It is *commonly argued* that Zoroastrianism shaped Jewish and Christian ideas of **heaven and hell, cosmic dualism, final judgment, bodily resurrection, a messiah, and linear history**, plausibly during the Persian period of Jewish contact. **This is genuinely contested, not settled:** the dating of Zarathushtra ranges from ~2000 BCE to the 4th c. BCE, and Zoroastrian texts were written down very late (Sasanian and after), so the *direction* and timing of influence are hard to pin down. Treat it as a strong hypothesis with real caveats.

## The Pattern

| | Origin | What we are | Where going | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Greek** | Chaos → Gaia → Olympians; autochthony | mortals seeking aretē / eudaimonia; *kleos*; reason | gloomy Hades; immortality via glory & offspring | **cyclical** |
| **Roman** | Troy / Aeneas, Romulus; divine destiny | *pietas* / *virtus*; collective over individual | *Roma Aeterna*, *imperium sine fine*; ancestor cult | empire as end-point |
| **Early Christian** | one God, *imago Dei*, the Fall | fallen souls redeemed by Christ; *agapē* / faith | resurrection, Last Judgment, Heaven / Hell | **linear** |
| **Persian / Zoroastrian** | Ahura Mazda's good creation | free agents in Asha vs. Druj; good thought/word/deed | Chinvat Bridge, Frashokereti, world perfected | **linear** |

The throughline: the Greco-Roman worldview is essentially **cyclical** — Rome's innovation was to offer "eternity" as the *permanence of the state* rather than a goal beyond it. The Persian and Christian schemes are **linear and goal-directed**, ending in a definitive judgment and a perfected, eternal state. That shift — from recurrence to destination, and from glory/duty to love/salvation — is the deepest movement in the story this series is tracing, and it is exactly the pivot Rome itself enacted when it replaced Homer's love with Virgil's piety (see [Homer vs. Vergil](homer_vergil_detail.md)).
