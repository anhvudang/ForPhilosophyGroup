# Will to Power - Main Points

## 1. Intellectual Background: Schopenhauer's Will to Life

Nietzsche's will to power was formulated as a direct competitor to Schopenhauer's will to life.

- Schopenhauer's will to life: a metaphysical basis underpinning all reality — blind, irrational, ever-striving
- Three characteristics: (1) there is a metaphysical will behind all of reality, (2) it is characterized by a struggle for existence, (3) this struggle causes suffering leading to pessimism
- The material world is merely an "objectification" of this will — we are effects, the will is the cause
- Universal conflict: every living thing maintains its existence by the constant destruction of another — "the will to live everywhere preys upon itself"

## 2. Intellectual Background: Darwinism

Darwin's materialism offered an alternative explanation to Schopenhauer's metaphysics.

- Darwin's "struggle for life" is caused by limited resources and natural selection — not metaphysical principles
- Materialism has no room for metaphysics ("beyond physics" cannot exist in a materialist framework)
- The central tension for Nietzsche: Is the will to power metaphysical (like Schopenhauer) or materialist (like Darwin)?
- This question has no simple answer — it changes throughout Nietzsche's intellectual development

## 3. Evolution of the Concept Through Nietzsche's Works

The will to power transforms from a psychological observation to a potential metaphysical principle.

### The Joyful Science — A Human Feeling
- First systematic treatment of power, but only about the "feeling of power" — a human affair
- Power is exercised over others by doing them good or doing them ill
- Restricted to humans: closer to Darwin than Schopenhauer, not metaphysical territory

### Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Creative Value-Making
- First use of the term "will to power"
- "Wherever I found a living thing, there found I will to power"
- Focused on value judgments: human systems of good and evil are expressions of will to power
- Assigning values is a creative act — an exercise of will to power
- Still primarily a human phenomenon, but extending to all organic life

### Beyond Good and Evil — The Thought Experiment
- Most references to will to power of any Nietzsche work (11 aphorisms)
- Presented as a thought experiment: what if the will to power explains ALL of reality, including the material world?
- "The world seen from within... would simply be will to power and nothing else"
- Still conditional language — "are we not permitted to make the attempt and ask the question?"

### Twilight of the Idols — Approaching Metaphysical Reality
- The time for thought experiments has passed — Nietzsche treats will to power as genuinely real
- Attacks philosophers who posit abstract concepts (God) at the top; instead our basest desires (will to power) are most real
- "Bottom up instead of top down" — will to power as the foundational reality, making God unnecessary
- The will to power now resembles a metaphysical principle, yet Nietzsche simultaneously attacks metaphysics itself

### The Antichrist — Power and Decadence
- "What is good? Whatever augments the feeling of power. What is evil? Whatever springs from weakness"
- "What is happiness? The feeling that power increases, that resistance is overcome"
- Focus on what happens when will to power is lacking: decadence, degeneration, weakness
- Life as "an instinct for growth, for survival, for the accumulation of forces, for power"

## 4. The Central Paradox

Nietzsche appears to hold contradictory positions: the will to power seems metaphysical, yet he attacks metaphysics.

- He rails against metaphysical dualism (Schopenhauer-style) while seeming to propose one
- A purely materialist framework cannot accommodate the will to power as he envisioned it in his late period
- Nietzsche himself struggled with this dilemma and could not resolve it before his mental illness

## 5. Reginster's Interpretation: Will to Overcoming Resistance

Bernard Reginster offers a resolution: the will to power is the will to the overcoming of resistance.

- Power is "devoid of any determinate content" — it gets content only from its relation to some specific desire or drive
- The will to power is a "second-order desire": whenever one desires something, one automatically desires the power to satisfy that desire
- Paradox: one actively wills resistance in order to overcome it (resistance is necessary for power to manifest)
- The will to power is insatiable — not self-defeating but perpetually growth-seeking
- Knowledge-seeking is another form of will to power: solving problems = overcoming resistance

## 6. Overcoming Schopenhauer's Pessimism

The will to power provides Nietzsche's escape from pessimistic nihilism.

- Schopenhauer: suffering is inevitable because the will is eternally insatiable → happiness is impossible → only escape is ascetic denial of the will
- Nietzsche's revaluation: suffering (= encountered resistance) becomes an opportunity for will to power, not a curse
- "What doesn't kill me makes me stronger" — pain is an opportunity to exercise power
- Even asceticism is a manifestation of will to power (the ascetic seeks resistance in the internal world to overcome it), not a denial of will
- Schopenhauer says "no" to the world; Nietzsche says "yes" — to both pleasures AND pains

## 7. The Unfinished Project

Nietzsche's philosophy remains deliberately incomplete.

- The will to power was intended as the centerpiece of a "reevaluation of all values"
- The Antichrist was the first book in a planned series for this revolution
- Mental illness prevented completion — "perhaps it is up to us to finish the task"
- The posthumous book "Will to Power" (compiled by his sister from notes) is not considered canonical
- The incompleteness is fitting: the will to power is never done — it IS the embrace of perpetual struggle
