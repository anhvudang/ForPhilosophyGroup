# 6. Recipes and wisdom: why cookbooks don't explain why

← [Back to Talk 14 README](README.md)

> Quotations are translated from the Vietnamese transcript. Original wording: [06_cong-thuc-va-tri-tue_vi.md](06_cong-thuc-va-tri-tue_vi.md)

---

## The idea

A cookbook gives you the formula, not the reason. It says "add this much salt, keep the heat here" but not what the salt does to the ingredients, or how that heat changes the dish.

And that is not a defect of cookbooks. It is **the general shape of all transmitted knowledge**: formulas copy, wisdom does not.

This is the most accessible statement of the Meaning / Ritual pair from [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md) — and notably, Vũ and Nhật, who argued throughout the session, both used this analogy and both agreed about it.

---

## Where it came from in the talk

It was not prepared. It surfaced near the end, out of three sources converging within a few minutes:

**Someone mentioned Tô Hoài.** The story of the Vietnamese writer Tô Hoài analyzing his own work, submitting it to a teacher, and being marked down for "not understanding the author's intent." Vũ: *"it would be fairly similar to that."*

**Thu mentioned a JLPT N1/N2 reading passage.** *"There was one about how cookbooks don't explain why people cook that way, they only supply the recipe."*

**Vũ said he had written a similar note that same day.** *"Today I had a similar note about cooking and living."*

Three independent sources converging on one point — usually a sign the idea is real.

---

## Two ways to know how to cook

### The wisdom route

> *"We put in this pile of ingredients and whatever we do it'll turn into some dish. And once we've cooked enough, we start to have some sense that this dish should go with this, or this much heat. It is wisdom. Internally we understand it, and we start cooking better dishes."*

Characteristics: slow, not transmissible in words, but **flexible** — when the ingredients change, you know how to adjust.

### The recipe route

> *"When we use what's called a recipe, it skips all of that — it's like: this much carrot, this much cabbage, this much heat, boom."*

Two problems Vũ named:
1. *"We can't be sure we have those ingredients."*
2. *"And we have no flexibility about what to do if this changes a little."*

The second is the real problem. A recipe is a function that is only correct on the input set it was written for. Outside that set it does not throw an error — it just produces a bad dish, and you don't know why.

---

## The jump: society's recipes

This is where the analogy becomes an idea:

> *"In some sense, a cooking recipe is like a definition of society. For instance: what a good man is, what a good woman is, what an accomplished person is, what a successful person is — those are recipes."*

And the misreading:

> *"In some sense those recipes are only a direction. But we understand them as: oh, we must do exactly this. And so instead of using the ingredients we have — which differ for each person — it's easier to go and do exactly this, even when we feel bad."*

Two words worth keeping: a recipe is a **direction**, not a **specification**. And *"even when we feel bad"* is where this connects to Fear — we keep following it even after the sense has reported an error, because abandoning the formula means taking responsibility yourself.

---

## The best detail: lemon and vinegar

A participant offered a small but sharp objection:

> *"Not everyone can tell that the sourness of lemon and the sourness of vinegar are the same."*

Vũ: *"I think anyone who cooks enough will be able to feel it."*

And another continued: *"before you make yourself learn, you still have to imitate someone."*

That brief exchange contains the whole structure of the problem:

- Wisdom **is** learnable, but only through repetitions.
- While you do not yet have wisdom, you **must** use the recipe. There is no other route.
- So the recipe is not the bad thing. It is mandatory at the start.

And that is why Nhật — who pushed back on Vũ all session — agrees here:

> *"At first we won't understand, like the cooking thing — if we don't quite understand we can follow the recipe. After we've been brought to understand, we'll have our own recipe."*

---

## The sharpest question of the talk, asked here

Nhật posed the question the whole model has to answer:

> *"Even if you've never cooked, when you see a recipe you'll want to imitate it rather than cook on your own, right? So **at what point do we know to follow the ritual — and at what point to follow the meaning?** Can we repudiate ritual?"*

This is the right question in the right place. If a recipe is mandatory at first and a trap later, then there must be a boundary — and where is it?

**Vũ's answer:**

> *"When we perform ritual, the purpose of performing ritual is to find out the meaning. And once we have the meaning, we start to be able to be more flexible. But the problem will be when we perform ritual and regard ritual as the correct thing."*

That is: there is no point in time. The distinguishing factor is **attitude** — are you performing the Ritual *in order to find the Meaning*, or *because the Ritual is correct*? The same action, two different states. (The same shape as the "one action, two motives" argument in [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md).)

**And Vũ followed with an observation about the question "is this right or wrong":**

> *"In our era the very common question is: is doing this right or wrong? And in general I think that's the wrong question — because asking it assumes there is some way, and that doing it that way makes it right. That question means there is some ritual that exists, and doing exactly that is enough, and we no longer care about the meaning part."*

This is the most practical application in the whole talk. It gives you a usable filter: **when you catch yourself asking "is this right or wrong," check whether the question is presupposing that a formula exists.**

## A parallel analogy: the Ship of Theseus

In the Q&A of phi2 there is another passage on the same subject, though it arrives in fragments: the **Ship of Theseus** (the transcript renders it "cesius"). Replace the rotten planks one by one; once all have been replaced, use the old rotten planks to build another ship — which is the real ship?

Vũ tied it to the same question: *"what is my essence?"* And a participant extended it to culture: *"if we transfer our entire culture into an AI, the AI doesn't have a body like ours."*

The passage was not developed (it was cut for time as the session moved to food and drink), but it is the same question: when the Ritual remains and the Meaning has gone, is what remains still the same thing?

---

## Why this idea is worth keeping

Because it is the version of [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md) that **requires nobody to believe in "Beauty."** It uses an example everyone has direct experience of, it requires no model of society, and it yields something you can act on:

- When you follow a life formula (what success is, what a good man or woman is), ask: **what ingredient set was this formula written for, and do my ingredients match?**
- And: am I doing this **to understand**, or **because it is correct**?

Neither question needs any new terminology.
