# 2. Rhetoric and dialectic: one tool, two directions

← [Back to Talk 14 README](README.md)

> Quotations are translated from the Vietnamese transcript. Original wording: [02_rhetoric-va-dialectic_vi.md](02_rhetoric-va-dialectic_vi.md)

---

## The idea

Rhetoric was the tool the Greeks used to *search for truth*. That same tool, unchanged, is also the perfect tool for indoctrination. The tool cannot tell which job it is doing — and what distinguishes the two jobs is not in the tool. It is whether the user is being **honest with himself**.

---

## The argument as given

### The two things a Greek wanted to learn

Vũ opened this section with a compact observation: in Greece before Macedon, a person who wanted to be famous had **two** skills to learn:

1. **Oratory** — persuading others with words
2. **The art of killing** — persuading others with swords and weapons

And the two greatest Greek heroes stand for exactly those two: **Achilles** is the art of violence, **Odysseus** is the art of rhetoric. *"Those are more or less Greece's two biggest heroes."*

> This pairing already appears in [Greek Civilization](../../HistoryAnalysis/010_Greek_DONE/readme.md) ("the two paths of persuasion"). Talk 14 adds the part that tracks how the Odysseus path **degrades**.

### What rhetoric is, and what it runs on

*"Oratory is usually someone standing on a platform, sort of preaching."* Vũ pointed out that it is entirely intact today: *"nearly all presidents stand on a platform and say this and that in order to persuade the people."*

Two operating characteristics:
- It **targets feeling** heavily.
- It uses very **flexible** language — meaning the sense of a word can be bent to the needs of the argument.

### Stage 1: rhetoric as a truth-finding tool

Early on, rhetoric was the instrument of the **sophists**, and they used it to argue with one another about what *eudaimonia* actually is:

> *"For example — is killing eudaimonia? Achilles killed a great many people and that's called eudaimonia, say. Why? Or if you don't kill people, then why is that eudaimonia?"*

At this stage the tool served the search. Vũ underlined: *"actually eudaimonia is the beauty I want to talk about"* — a direct bridge to [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md).

### Stage 2: while Athens rose, nobody asked anything

*"On the way up, nobody asks why we have this. Everyone just goes: oh, we have this, let's learn it."*

A small but very sharp point: **success switches off checking.** The tool has not degraded yet, but the mechanism for auditing it has stopped running.

### Stage 3: rhetoric becomes an instrument of indoctrination

When Athens declined, the same tool was exploited. And Vũ stated the double edge plainly:

> *"For us to improve anything, we have to be really, truly honest with ourselves. Rhetoric is a tool for finding truth. But it is also a tool we can use to brainwash other people and produce terrible decisions."*

Note the attached condition: **honesty with oneself**. Without it the tool still runs — it just runs in the wrong direction. This is the same condition Vũ will re-impose in the section on fear.

### Socrates: the immune response

Once Athens had got bad enough, Socrates appeared. What he did was not to offer a doctrine but to **break** other people's rhetoric by asking crooked questions. Vũ's example:

> — Is stealing good?
> — No, stealing isn't good.
> — What if I steal from an enemy?
> — Oh... then it's good.

*"He keeps doing that for a while and the other man gets completely tangled and says things that conflict with each other."* The practical result: *"about 30 minutes later, that person is very angry."*

Worth noticing: Socrates does not prove his opponent **wrong**. He proves him **inconsistent**. That is a different criterion — and it is the criterion dialectic will adopt.

### Dialectic: a far more logical system

After Socrates, Greece produced **dialectic**. *"We'd say it's closer to what we now call science."*

The mechanism Vũ described, whose commonest instance is mathematics:

> *"We take a proposition, and for this to hold, A and B both have to be true. Then prove A, prove B."*

That is: decompose a claim into conditions, then handle each condition. Entirely unlike standing on a platform aiming at feeling.

### Aristotle reorders the hierarchy

This is Aristotle's contribution, and a concrete instance of systematization (see [file 01](01_systematization-as-engine.md)):

| | Under Plato | Under Aristotle |
|---|---|---|
| Dialectic | Coming into use; is logical thinking | The **only** thing permitted to define truth |
| Rhetoric | Still one path of inquiry | Only the instrument of **propagation** once truth is found |

In the original: *"dialectic is the only thing permitted to define truth... and rhetoric is only what comes after we've found that truth — then rhetoric is the thing that spreads it out, and it's only a tool for transmission."*

---

## Why this idea is worth keeping

It gives a way of reading the present that needs no new vocabulary. Social media, politics, marketing, and the arguments inside this very group are all rhetoric running. The useful question is not "is this rhetoric or dialectic" — it is:

**Is the speaker using this tool to find out what is true, or to defend a conclusion already held?**

And the frightening part is that the speaker usually does not know. Vũ applied it to himself in the same session: *"this talk today has a part that is me wanting to share this knowledge, and also some part of it that is wanting to defend some image of myself."*

That is the correct use of this idea: not to label other people, but to audit yourself.

---

## Notes, and one thing to correct

**Mixed terminology.** In the talk, Vũ uses "dialectic" for two slightly different things: (a) dialectic in the Socratic sense — approaching truth through dialogue and the detection of contradiction — and (b) logical inference / proof on the mathematical model. In classical Greece these two were nearer each other than they are now, but carried into the modern setting they need separating — because "science today" is really (b) plus experiment, not (a).

**One strength omitted.** Rhetoric is not only "aiming at feeling." In the Greek tradition it comprises logos (argument), pathos (feeling) and ethos (the speaker's standing). Vũ's presentation collapses it to pathos, which sharpens the contrast with dialectic but also makes it easier than the reality.

**This section is consistent with a later one.** Aristotle's demotion of rhetoric to "merely the instrument of transmission" is a systematizing decision — and it reproduces exactly the structure of "Meaning / Ritual" in [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md): find it first (Meaning), then transmit it (Ritual). Vũ did not connect the two in the talk, but they are the same shape.
