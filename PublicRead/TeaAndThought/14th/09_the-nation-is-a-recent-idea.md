# 9. "Country" is a very recent idea

← [Back to Talk 14 README](README.md)

> Quotations are translated from the Vietnamese transcript. Original wording: [09_dat-nuoc-la-mot-y-tuong-moi_vi.md](09_dat-nuoc-la-mot-y-tuong-moi_vi.md)

---

## The idea

What we call "country" — in the sense of *I am Vietnamese, I have a responsibility to this country, I fight for this country* — is a very recent invention. About 250 years old.

Before that, people identified by **village**, by **the land they were born in**, by **the king they paid taxes to**. Not by nation.

> *"In the old days people identified with the land they were born in, not with the country... People would identify as: I was born in this village, son of this man, this area. But only much later did the country take a larger position in each person's thinking: I am Vietnamese. Whereas in the old days it was, I am of such-and-such village."*

---

## The initial claim, and why it was too strong

Vũ first stated it in a very strong form:

> *"The idea of the country is an idea France built up about 300-odd years ago. It is very new; people only imagine it is old. Before that, Đại Nam did not exist at all, the country of Việt did not exist, the country of China did not exist — we were simply those tracts of land paying taxes to the king in Beijing or in Hanoi."*

And it was objected to immediately in the room: *"you're confusing the concept of a republic with the concept of a nation."*

---

## The tightening — this is the part worth keeping

What is valuable about this idea is not the opening claim but **how it was tightened across three rounds of objection**.

### Round 1: separate "nation" from "nation-state"

Participant: *"the concept of a nation builds up across the whole of history."*

Vũ agreed and revised: *"I understand that, I agree with that. The nation here is the **nation-state** — an idea that leans more toward government and toward institutions."*

### Round 2: separate "the country exists" from "identity with the country"

Participant: *"the country already existed."*

Vũ: *"yes, so the two concepts are different. The country is very old, but **identity** with the country is new."*

This is the correct version. The opening claim ("the country of Việt did not exist") is false; this one ("identity with the country is new") holds.

### Round 3: say exactly which identity is new

Vũ restated it one last time, most precisely:

> *"I'd say the identity that **I am a person with responsibility to this country** is new. Previously it was: I have responsibility to something else."*

That is the point. It is not that "country" is new. It is that **obligation to the country as a source of identity** is new.

---

## The evidence: the French Revolution

Vũ identified a specific turning point:

> *"Previously people fought for [the king], say. The French thing is the first time people fought for something called a country. In the old days we'd go and fight and say 'ah, for this king we go.' That was the first time people said 'ah, because the country needs me, and I fight for this country.'"*

This is a **testable** criterion: rather than asking "did the country exist," ask **"who are people fighting for?"** For a person (the king), or for an abstract entity (the nation)?

And the shift from the first to the second is a historical event with a date.

### The philosophical groundwork: Locke

Vũ connected to a chain of intellectual causation (the transcript renders it "john john," i.e. **John Locke**):

> *"Locke talks a great deal about the republic and about property rights, and the role of the state in protecting the people's property rights. So Locke and the [thinkers] of that period began talking a lot about [that]."*

And on the relation among nation, state and republic: *"it's more or less the same [period] — the two go together, each building on the other."*

---

## A natural demonstration: the talk itself

There is something interesting in the transcript that nobody in the room pointed out, but which is living evidence for this idea.

The introductions at the start of the session (phi2) turned into a long discussion about **province names and provincial mergers**: Bắc Giang / Bắc Ninh, Bắc Thái (= Bắc Kạn + Thái Nguyên), Hà Bắc, Hà Nam Ninh, Hoàng Liên Sơn, Bình Trị Thiên, Hà Tây folded into Hanoi, Phú Nhuận demoted to a ward. Someone said *"I've lost my home province."* Someone said *"my birth certificate says Bắc Thái province"* — a province that no longer exists.

And the stories they told about themselves were about their parents' **ancestral** home, the village, the district, their accent (someone guessed a home province from a Hà Tĩnh accent), their kinship (*"Hùng's paternal grandmother is my paternal grandfather's elder sister"*).

That is: administrative units change constantly and people experience it as loss, but **village and lineage do not change**. The old identity is still there, sitting underneath the national one. Exactly as Vũ said — it just surfaced somewhere nobody was looking.

---

## What it explains

This idea does not stand alone — it is the missing piece of [file 07](07_ruling-through-the-elite.md).

Why did the city-states Alexander took not resist? Because there was nothing to resist **for**:

> *"The city-state back then didn't have that big a sense of identity. A city-state back then was like: Alexander's fine, the Persian Empire's fine — whoever taxes me better, I like him better; whoever doesn't behead me, I follow him."*

If you are a person of a village and not a person of a nation, then a distant king changing names is the king's business. It does not touch you.

It also explains why ancient conquest was **easier** than modern conquest: not because of weapons, but because the conquered of antiquity had no abstraction to be loyal to.

Vũ also flagged one case requiring caution: *"China and Japan did have a high concept of ethnicity."* And settled at the right level: *"each person's identity would not be governed by the country to the degree it is today."* That is, not a binary of present/absent, but a matter of **degree of governance**.

---

## Why this idea is worth keeping

Because it turns something that feels eternal into something with a birth date.

If "I am Vietnamese" is an identity constructed over roughly 250 years, then it can change again. It can weaken (globalization, migration — and note that most people in that room were living in Japan), or be displaced by some other unit of identification.

And this connects straight back to [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md): "fighting for the country" is a **Ritual**. It had a very specific **Meaning** when it was born in revolutionary France. The question is whether the Meaning is intact, or only the Ritual remains. Vũ used almost exactly this example when speaking about "fighting for freedom": *"applied to the present circumstance, something isn't right, and we can't explain it."*

## Verification notes

- **"300-odd years"** — inaccurate. The French Revolution is 1789, i.e. about **236 years** as of 2025. If you include the pre-revolutionary intellectual phase (Locke died 1704), "over 300 years" can be made to work for the ideas but not for the nation-state. Use ~250 years.
- **"The idea of the country was built by France"** — too narrow. The modern nation-state is usually taken to form through a sequence: the Peace of Westphalia (1648, state sovereignty), the American and French Revolutions (sovereignty of the people), then nineteenth-century nationalism (German and Italian unification). France is the most important turning point for **identity**, but not the sole source.
- **"The country of Việt did not exist, China did not exist"** — false if taken to mean political entities. Đại Việt, Đại Nam, and the Chinese dynasties were all political entities with borders, apparatus, and a sense of themselves. Vũ did retract this in the talk when challenged; the revised version (*"the country is very old, identity with the country is new"*) is the correct one. **Use the revised version, not the first.**
- **"China and Japan had a high concept of ethnicity"** — right in direction; both have a long tradition of distinguishing "us / outsiders" predating the Western nation-state model. But the modern nation-state in Japan is a product of the Meiji era (1868), so still modern.
- **Locke** — Vũ's description of Locke (property rights, the state's role in protecting them) is accurate. But Locke writes about **limited government and the social contract**, not directly about *the nation*; the "loyalty to the nation" piece is closer to Rousseau and the French revolutionary thinkers.
