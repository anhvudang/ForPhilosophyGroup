# 8. Why small armies beat large ones

← [Back to Talk 14 README](README.md)

> Quotations are translated from the Vietnamese transcript. Original wording: [08_doi-quan-khong-bao-gio-chay_vi.md](08_doi-quan-khong-bao-gio-chay_vi.md)

---

## The idea

An ancient battle does not end when one side has been killed. It ends when one side **loses its will to fight and runs**. Casualty rates were surprisingly low — the winner lost around 5%, the loser around 10%, a heavy defeat around 20%.

Which means the real military objective was not destruction. It was **breaking morale**. And from that follows a counter-intuitive consequence: a small army that never runs can beat one twice its size.

> *"If we have 300 soldiers against 1,000 and the 300 never run, the 300 always win."*

---

## The mechanism: anvil and hammer

The tactic the Macedonian army had mastered (the transcript renders it "ambient hammer," i.e. **anvil and hammer**):

1. The two infantry lines engage → both sides are **locked in place**, unable to move.
2. Cavalry wheels around behind the locked infantry block.
3. Continuous pressure on the rear ranks.
4. *"Until the infantry inside break — they simply have more or less no will to fight left, and they run."*
5. *"And once they've run, that army disintegrates very fast."*

Note that the decisive step is step 4, and it is a **psychological** step, not a lethal one. The locked infantry are not dying — they just cannot see a way out.

---

## Low casualty rates, and what that tells you

Vũ gave the numbers and explained them:

> *"That's part of why casualty rates in medieval battles weren't actually that high. In a typical engagement the winner loses about 5% of its soldiers, the loser about 10%, and a very heavy defeat about 20%."*

There was a clarifying exchange in the talk — a participant asked about the comparison with today:

- **Absolute numbers:** more die today. *"Today a battle is both larger and deadlier."*
- **Rate:** higher today too, because of machine guns. *"Charging into machine guns."*
- **Duration:** an ancient battle *"was over in a single afternoon"*; a modern battle lasts far longer.

And what happens to the losing side: *"if you lose, you run... totally scattered, all formations lost. If you can run into the forest you escape. Otherwise the other side chases and kills; wherever there are a lot of soldiers clustered they just chase in."*

So most actual casualties occurred **after** the battle had been decided — during the pursuit. That is an important correction to the usual picture of ancient warfare.

---

## Darius III: the inverse proof

At both Issus and Gaugamela, Alexander did not break the Persian army. He broke **one man**.

Issus: *"it turned out Darius III was rather a timid man, and so he ran. And because he ran, suddenly all the soldiers here went 'what am I doing here when my king has already run?'"*

And Vũ stated the most memorable part:

> *"Even though they had the overwhelming numbers — meaning if they had just kept fighting they would certainly have won — because Darius III had run, that army disintegrated extremely fast."*

Gaugamela repeated it exactly: Alexander drove straight at Darius; Darius fled again.

The consequence afterwards was not military but political: *"after this battle Darius III more or less lost power entirely, because he assembled two armies and ran both times, abandoned his soldiers both times, and very many died — so even if he was nominally still king, people would no longer follow him."*

---

## Parmenion: the overlooked half of three victories

This is the best part of this file, because it is the part nobody tells when telling the story of Alexander.

In all three battles the left wing was commanded by **Parmenion**. It was thin. It won no victory. And it **never ran**.

Asked whether Parmenion's unit did anything, Vũ explained the full mechanism:

> *"One of the reasons the victory was possible is that Parmenion's unit more or less never ran — meaning they would more or less fight to the death. The thing is, when you run, the enemy force then has time to [regroup] the units that haven't run yet. But because this man's unit didn't run, they kept it locked there — even though they were exhausted — and the force fighting them couldn't switch over to [regroup] the [enemy] units that hadn't yet run."*

Restated: **not running = pinning the enemy = the enemy never gets a free hand.** That is a genuine tactical contribution; it just does not look like a feat of arms.

And Vũ stressed that it was the precondition for everything: *"even though Alexander was more or less a military genius, there's something we don't talk about much — Alexander's left wing was relatively thin but they never ran."*

### And from there: why modern armies shoot deserters

Vũ carried the mechanism into the present, coldly:

> *"That's why we have army corps today — it's that if you run you get shot. Why? Because if you run it is certain death. That's how it is in an army."*

The logic: if one person running causes the whole unit to disintegrate, then the price of running must be set higher than the price of staying. A brutal solution, but it addresses exactly the problem above.

---

## The real source of that army: Philip II

A point Vũ emphasized, and one that matters for assigning credit correctly:

> *"Philip II had [built] an army that was extremely loyal, extremely professional, and willing to die for something."*

Three characteristics Vũ named for the Macedonian army:
1. **A full-time professional force** — not farmers called up when war came. *"Their job was to hack and to kill, that's all; they were really soldiers."*
2. **Mastery of anvil and hammer.**
3. **The elite "companion" cavalry** — heavy horsemen, thick armor, *"not afraid to charge anyone."*

And Parmenion is evidence of one more thing: **promotion on merit.** *"This Parmenion was more or less a commoner at the start, and a commoner being promoted in the army was extremely rare — and he became a general under Philip II."*

Vũ summed up the difference between father and son: Philip *"built everything from zero"* out of a territory that was *"more or less a failed state"*; Alexander inherited the most powerful army in the world, and *"Alexander's use of troops and risk-taking was extremely different from Philip's."*

---

## The asymmetry of risk

This is the final point, and the one most worth keeping:

> *"Persia had one privilege: when they lost they could reassemble — lose, reassemble, lose, reassemble. They lost three major battles and could still reassemble. Whereas in any battle, if Alexander lost, Alexander was finished."*

In other words: Alexander won three times but was never permitted to lose once. Darius lost three times and was still nominally king.

And that makes Alexander's behavior — always charging at the front, always taking extreme risk — harder to understand, not easier. Vũ reached for a comparison: *"it's like Elon Musk today — very intelligent but also very reckless, and doing things nobody understands."* Plus a notion he called *magnetic*: *"the feeling that you are the one God sent down to do something, and Alexander was not afraid."*

There is an implicit link here to [file 05](05_beauty-meaning-ritual-fear.md): Alexander acted out of something that was not risk calculation. Whatever we call it.

---

## Why this idea is worth keeping

Because it is a model of the **breaking point**, not a model of warfare.

A system does not collapse from losing enough resources. It collapses when the people inside it stop believing there is a way out. And that can happen while the system still has resources to spare — exactly like the Persian army at Issus, still twice as large and still disintegrating.

Practical consequence: what needs protecting in an organization under pressure is not resources but the belief that nobody will run first. That is what Parmenion supplied, and what Darius destroyed.

## Verification notes

- **The 5% / 10% / 20% figures** — a reasonable general estimate in direction (pre-modern battle casualties were lower than intuition suggests, with most occurring in the pursuit), but should not be cited as precise data. No source was given in the talk.
- **All three battles outnumbered 2:1** — right in direction, but the specific figures given in the talk (40,000 Persians at Granicus with 20,000 cavalry; 120,000+ at Gaugamela) are all modern estimates from varying sources with wide spreads. The ancient figure ("one million soldiers") Vũ himself rejected: *"nobody believes that at all."*
- **Gaugamela: Darius had the battlefield levelled** — correct, and Vũ's reason is correct too: Persia was strong in cavalry, and flat ground favors cavalry.
- **Scythed chariots and war elephants at Gaugamela** — attested. Vũ is also right that they had little effect.
- **"Darius III was timid"** — this is the winner's reading and deserves caution. A king withdrawing when his personal line is pierced may be calculating dynastic preservation, not merely frightened. Everything we know about Darius comes through Greek sources — exactly as the talk itself warned in [file 04](04_legitimacy-is-manufactured.md): *"that's the history written by the winning side."*
